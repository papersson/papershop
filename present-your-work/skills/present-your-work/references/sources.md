# Where the rules come from

Each rule in the skill traces to a source below or to the case in `case-eventlearn.md`. The sources
are summarized only as far as the skill uses them.

## The thesis defense

A doctoral defense puts a body of work to a committee that did not do it, and the committee's
questions have a stable shape: what problem the work addresses and why it matters, what it
contributes and how that compares with what was known, how the claims are supported, where they
stop, and what should follow. A defense is judged on whether the candidate can state the
contribution plainly and place it, not on how much was done. The skill takes the genre and its
five questions from this practice, with one change for autonomous work: "what should follow" is
ranked and costed, because the presentation is a checkpoint and the reader decides the next stretch.

## The Heilmeier catechism

George H. Heilmeier, director of DARPA from 1975 to 1977, asked every proposal a fixed set of
questions: what are you trying to do, articulated with no jargon; how is it done today and what are
the limits of current practice; what is new in your approach and why will it succeed; who cares; if
you succeed, what difference will it make; what are the risks; how much will it cost; how long will
it take; what are the mid-term and final exams that check for success. The skill uses it for the
card's Goal (stated without jargon), for Distance (the exams), for Obstacles (the risks) and for
ranking the next steps by expected gain and cost.

## Minto's pyramid principle

Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking* (first published 1987).
A reader takes in ideas most easily when the governing answer comes first and the supporting ideas
follow, grouped beneath it, each group summarized by the idea above it; the introduction moves from
situation to complication to question to answer. The skill uses it for the one-sentence Answer, for
the card as the top of the pyramid, and for putting the point first in every section and paragraph.

## Gopen and Swan

George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", *American Scientist* 78,
no. 6 (1990): 550 to 558. Readers expect the start of a sentence (the topic position) to hold the
person or thing whose story it is and the information that links back, and the end (the stress
position) to hold what is new and to be emphasized; they expect a verb soon after the subject, and
context before anything new. The skill uses these expectations in step 5.

## Mayer's principles of multimedia learning

Richard E. Mayer, *Multimedia Learning* (2001; third edition 2020). Of his principles, three carry
over to text and all of them to the video: coherence (people learn better when extraneous material
is left out), signaling (when cues mark the organization of the essential material) and
pre-training (when they know the names and characteristics of the main concepts before the main
explanation). The skill uses coherence for the subtraction pass and the budgets, signaling for the
answer-first structure and finding-stating headings, and pre-training for the background chain:
each idea is introduced before the argument uses it.

## Olah and Carter, "Research Debt"

Chris Olah and Shan Carter, "Research Debt", *Distill* (2017). Poor exposition, undigested ideas
and private notation accumulate as a debt that every later reader pays in effort; distillation,
the work of explaining, is research work in its own right and is cheaper to do as the research
happens. The skill uses it for the research-time pages, which distill as the work goes, and for
treating the presentation as work with its own steps rather than a summary written at the end.

## The curse of knowledge

Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein and Martin Weber, "The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings:
An Experimental Analysis", *Journal of Political Economy* 97, no. 5 (1989): 1232 to 1254, named the
effect: people who know something cannot set that knowledge aside when predicting what others know.
Elizabeth Newton's tapping study (Stanford doctoral dissertation, 1990) showed its size: people who
tapped the rhythm of well-known songs predicted that listeners would name about half of them, and
listeners named 3 of 120. Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014), chapter 3, makes it the main
cause of unclear writing, through private labels, skipped steps and abstraction. The skill uses it
for three rules: the writer cannot find the work's labels by rereading, so a glossary kept while
the labels are coined and a script do it; the writer's sense that a text is clear is no evidence,
so a cold reader checks it; and each idea gets a concrete example before its name.

## The teach-back

The name comes from clinical communication, where a patient explains back in their own words what
the clinician said, and the clinician treats a wrong retelling as a fault in the explanation, not
in the patient (Dean Schillinger and colleagues, "Closing the Loop", *Archives of Internal
Medicine* 163, no. 1 (2003): 83 to 90). The skill keeps that assignment of fault: a cold reader's
wrong retelling is fixed in the draft's structure.

## The case

check of their process and lost the one reader who mattered. Each of its six causes left a rule.
