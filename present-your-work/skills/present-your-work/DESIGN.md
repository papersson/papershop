# Why present-your-work is shaped this way

Agents are good at finishing work and poor at handing it back. The default ending is an activity
log: accurate, chronological, full of the session's own names, and silent on what the reader most
needs, which is what is now true, why it was done this way, and what they have to do next. Each choice below answers part of that, and each records what it gives up.

## Exposition, not an audit

A report of finished work could try to do two things: make the work understood, and prove it is
right. They pull against each other. Proof (test runs, commands, how each claim is known) pushes
the point down the page and turns an explanation into a list of credentials, and in a
conversation the reader can ask for it in one line. So the skill does only the first. It says
what is true, why it was done this way and what the key idea is, and it leaves proof out unless
the reader asked or the catch is that something didn't work. The card still asks for what is
opaque, because a result nobody understands has delivered little of what matters. It gives up the
reassurance of a report that shows its working.

## In the chat, in under a minute

The reader is in the conversation, knows the request and is waiting. A file, a page or a review
step would cost them more than the presentation saves, and a check that takes minutes would not be
run at the end of every task. So quick mode writes from what is already in context, runs one
deterministic script that takes under a second, and delivers in the chat. It gives up the second
reader that a longer presentation would earn.

## A card before the prose

A writer who starts from the session reproduces its order and its names. Filling seven short lines
first (goal, answer, why, key idea, the catch, opaque, next) forces the choice of what matters
before any sentence can carry the session's shape in. In quick mode the card stays in the writer's
head; its lines become the paragraphs.

## The catch beside the answer

A single limitations section keeps hedges out of every sentence, which readers learn to skip. But
the one limitation that changes what the reader should do is the first thing they need after the
answer, and a list at the end is where decisive facts go unread. So the skill says the catch once
beside the answer and drops caveats that carry no information.

## A voice that does not read as machine-written

A report that reads like a model wrote it gets skimmed, so the voice is part of the job. Most of
it is decided while drafting: an editing pass afterwards removes only part of the tells and
starts rewriting good sentences once it removes more. So the skill puts the voice rules in the
drafting step, shows three reports in the target voice instead of a long list of bans, asks for
one light reread as the listener, and lets the script count the structures that give a draft away.
Translating through another dialect or a second model changes the words and keeps the structure,
and in agent reports the structure (labelled sentences, colon reveals) is most of the problem. It
gives up the speed of a template: a report in this voice cannot be filled in like a form.

## A script for what the writer cannot see

The writer cannot find their own private names by rereading, cannot feel their own length, and
tends to praise their own work. A deterministic script finds coined-looking names, superlatives,
the markers of a log and the structures of machine-written prose, gives the same answer twice and
costs nothing to rerun. It cannot judge whether the answer is right or the catch
is the right one, so it is a floor, not a verdict. Its heuristics will flag some ordinary names;
`--known` and code spans take those out, and an over-flag costs a glance while a missed one costs
the reader.

## Budgets

Sixty to three hundred words, two new terms and three numbers outside code spans are judgement,
not measurement: what a reader takes in at a glance at the end of a task. The floor matters as
much as the ceiling, since readers dislike over-compressed text as much as padded text. They are
the first thing to revise when real presentations show them wrong.
