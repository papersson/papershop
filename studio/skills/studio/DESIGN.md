# Why Studio keeps these parts

## The brief decides the explanation

The same tools can produce a useful undergraduate explanation or an overwhelming catalogue.
Viewer level, a few ideas and a small recurring example change the result more than additional
review machinery. The story session therefore gets deliberate time with the user. A one-page
brief keeps those choices visible, and a reel of boards held to the actual voice makes density
and breathing room perceptible before scene code is expensive to change.

Story starts with Elicit's model-derived panel and rich brief, saved in `research/elicit.md`.
The user's request wins over both that material and taste; the user's recorded taste wins over
Elicit's general knowledge. Conversion keeps canonical lecture order, a small example, performed hero moments and explicit
omissions: cut scope, never craft. Three or four ideas is guidance, not a cap; numbers and
caveats can overwhelm even a narrow story. A governing question and protagonist are optional. Chapter jobs receive only the relevant
hero description and panel principles from `research/story/<clip>.md`. Without the installed
skill, a short local method supplies those same questions without an installation stop.

The 3:02 hash-table proof reached a playable cut 7m11s after delegated story lock. Its converted
Elicit brief and fallback brief kept essentially the same mechanism and remembered moments;
the sequential same-agent comparison does not establish Elicit's superiority. The raw material
was useful but did not prevent two crowded transitions. One elicitation and Studio's existing
review pass suffice; a longer raw brief is not permission to add a second production process.

The 0.15 hash-table film exposed a taste failure despite the fast production: the user found its
visuals lame, its sound effects cheap, its governing question forced and some holds too long.
The loved sphere opening, Blender hard drive and tunnel game-feel pass show why austerity was
the wrong correction. After story lock, one style-frame step now sets a finished visual target:
three to five actual-engine frames beside a reference of the same kind: 3Blue1Brown for abstract
subjects, Ciechanowski or Kurzgesagt for physical ones. Drawing style, typography, palette,
composition and cameras become chapter inputs; admitted physical subjects also use materials and light. This is preparation, not another
review stage or permission stop; existing delegation applies. The existing chapter review checks
that the film matches the frames.

There are two decisions: the user locks the story, then says when to ship. Routine construction
and corrections proceed within those decisions. The skill is a router for story, shoot and desk;
five references supply taste, collaboration and the tools needed for each job.

## Taste is durable context

A model can write an animation and describe good teaching. It cannot infer the user's specific
preferences from first principles. `references/taste.md` retains those preferences with their
reasons: canonical level, a toy example, full explanatory sentences, a clean overview, close-ups,
performed events and time to read what changed. Holds follow content, not fixed minimums;
a long unchanged picture can drag just as adjacent introductions can crowd. No prediction quota
forces a lecture to invent questions or pauses. Its final section keeps new lessons tied to actual viewer notes.
The user's request supplies any departure needed for a particular video.

## One timeline keeps picture and sound together

Narration, words, captions, clips and cues share a frame grid. Every frame is a function of time,
so a still, a rendered chapter and the live desk show the same state. A named event times the
picture and its sound on one frame. Recorded sounds use their transient peak rather than their
file's start. The beat sheet makes contact, consequence and settling part of the authored story.

Chapter cues are separate source files merged by one timeline build. Each chapter agent has its
own scene and data directory. Private check workspaces let agents inspect their work concurrently
without replacing the shared timeline or each other's cache. The main session applies shared
changes and makes the combined cut once the chapters are ready.

## Fast versions keep the user involved

The user wants to see the work, give a note and see its effect in minutes. The live engine draws
plain JavaScript scenes directly on the desk and hot-reloads them. The same renderer captures
stills and movies. Incremental keys include scene dependencies, data, assets, layout and each
chapter's relative timeline, so a changed chapter can render alone. Cached narration preserves
unchanged paragraphs. Draft cuts keep feedback fast; the shipping decision requests the final.

The desk records notes on the sentence and saved cut the user saw, replies beneath them and keeps
earlier versions available. HTTP range requests preserve video seeking. Whole-story feedback in
chat complements sentence notes. Suggestions from reviewers remain visibly separate until the
user accepts them. A reviewer cannot substitute for the viewer's judgment of level or pace.

## Reviews provide evidence

The story panel reads once through three parallel perspectives: expert, viewer and editor.
Each gets the supplied brief and script in an empty working folder to reduce context leakage.
That directory is isolation of context, not a security sandbox. Missing perspectives are reported.
Findings remain plain files and the user sees unresolved questions.

After the first scene cut, one reviewer per chapter considers motion and frames together, in
parallel. Each bundle samples the saved cut and includes sound evidence around its events.
The findings become suggested desk notes. A changed script or an applied correction never
invalidates permission to continue. The checks remain independent of a reviewer's verdict.

## Voice and sound are assets

Kokoro paragraph synthesis carries intonation across sentences. A fixed voice and speed keep a
video coherent and avoid retiming every word late in production. The lexicon preserves deliberate
pronunciations. Voice checking caught missing synthesized sentences, and alignment gives effects
and labels useful word anchors. ElevenLabs remains an explicitly selected option with cached
responses, so a rebuild can reproduce paid speech.

Sound defaults to an empty list. In Burtt’s sense, recorded material sounds and typed-key streams
make actual touches tactile: keys, buttons, windows and real physical contacts. Abstract arrivals,
reveals and bucket operations stay silent, even when a physical metaphor depicts them. Shoot
jobs carry exact allowed events by chapter; the main session retains control of the mix.
Recorded CC0 material sounds, key recordings and typed-code streams remain available.
Typing replays actual file versions and uses the same keystroke events for picture and sound.
The mix bus supplies shared room, presence and ducking. A fixed whole-track loudness gain and
true-peak limiter preserve quiet pauses; dynamic normalization once lifted room noise between
sentences. Audio checks measure sync, audible effects, masking, pause space and loudness.
Listening still supplies judgments those measurements cannot make.

## The picture has room to explain

Captions occupy a reserved band because covering the subject makes the explanation harder to
read. They are chunked from the narration with phrase breaks and a minimum reading time. Stage
labels identify things. A clean map and large close-ups keep detail from accumulating indefinitely.
One colour retains one meaning, and outer contours carry more weight than internal dividers.

Part D's lit hash-table cabinet failed the user's taste. Its brief confused a rich physical
illustration with good abstract explanation. The hard rule is restored: 3D only when “the viewer
needs to see ___, and the flat diagram hides it.” Abstract subjects almost never pass. Their bar
is 3Blue1Brown: a calm dark ground, meaningful palette, confident vector lines, beautiful moving
type, a large mechanism, continuous transformations and varied close-ups, overviews and comparisons.
An exactly mapped physical metaphor can be drawn flat, never modelled and lit. No metaphor is required.

For physical subjects that pass the test, live 3D shares the timeline, legend and checks with SVG.
The lit look and Blender plates reveal real form, contact and occlusion at the Ciechanowski bar.
Projected labels stay live and readable. A plate probe measures its cost before a long render. Live also supplies recorded code panels, terminals, the typed editor and its program
window. Code-view folds, highlights, collapse and helper cards share the same pure replay model.
One renderer now supplies both the desk and the movie; there is no separate scene registry or bundle.

## Checks catch specific bugs

Length checks caught disagreement between Python and JavaScript at half-frame boundaries.
Determinism checks catch state that depends on earlier frames. Bounds, band, contrast and
legibility checks catch clipped or unreadable elements. In-frame and occlusion warnings expose
narrated objects that the picture hides. Colour-legend checks catch meanings that change colour.
Motion and flash checks inspect rendered frames. Pacing warns on both crowded moves and stage
intervals over three seconds without visible new content, including opening and tail. It removes
only the actual marked prediction interval, crops captions away and discounts ambient motion.
Small readable label arrivals count. Pixel changes cannot prove semantic novelty: ornamental
movement is not a valid fix, and the existing review still judges reading demand. Asset provenance and code-view checks preserve
source relationships. Some checks are heuristic warnings; the story explains whether a hold or
an overlap is intentional.

Setup is inspectable through preflight and doctor. The launcher enters a pinned environment and
caches it to avoid repeated startup cost. Exports keep separate layouts and cache keys while
sharing the timeline. Publish produces the final master, web copy, poster and colophon from that
same source. The user's shipping decision is one line with a name and time.

## Measure the whole loop

File locks prevent competing shared writes by waiting. Stage timing measures elapsed production
and user waiting without limiting the work. The standing ablation prepares matching starts for
a bare agent and Studio, with the same topic, model and length. The useful comparison is the
videos and the user's verdict, alongside the time to reach them. A tool earns its place through
what it contributes to that result. The shaders proof reached the first playable in 10m43s after delegated story lock. The user’s
assessment remains distinct from the agent’s frame comparison; measured time does not establish taste.


## The desk shows the film taking shape

The desktop review bay gives the viewer most of the screen, with story, picture, voice, sound,
notes and chapter-agent lanes beneath it and a note inspector beside it. Picture thumbnails replace
the stills wall. Keyboard transport, captions, speed and same-time A/B let the user stay in the film.
Notes attach to a moment, range, beat, whole video or lane and enter the durable inbox immediately.
Reading a note does not acknowledge it; starting and resolving it are explicit. This prevents a
watcher restart or a dropped connection from silently losing work. Suggestions remain accept/dismiss
choices. New cuts arrive through server events, and the desk is a detached process with its own log.

The event log records real file saves, stage marks, checks, renders and explicit agent activity.
`studio event` is the common entry point for agents and hooks. File observations at startup do not
pretend to be activity. A lane becomes quiet at 30 seconds without an event and stalled at two minutes,
retaining its last action. Checks sit at their actual second; fixes turn the mark green. A completed
version changes the title and gently pulses play once. ETA comes from measured history and says when
it is a guess. A chapter’s first saved scene replaces its board and can play before the full cut lands.

Transcript adapters are opt-in, scoped to one known lane and limited to tool/start/completion
metadata. Prompts, reasoning and command contents stay out of the desk. Stop is a durable cooperative
control, shown as requested until the agent acknowledges it. It does not imply that an unknown process
was killed. Replay preserves event order and time; historical picture reproduction requires archived
source snapshots. The first recorded shoot has current chapter pictures at historical save events,
so it proves event behaviour but cannot reproduce every intermediate pixel.

Claude's plugin hooks now report work without repeated event calls. Shoot writes one small active
record per STUDIO_HOME with unique lane jobs, launch tags, session/agent bindings and a six-hour
expiry. A tagged background Agent/Task result binds the returned agent id; newer tool payloads can
also bind on the child's first Read of its unique job. SubagentStart alone lacks the description,
so parallel starts are never paired by order. See the [Claude hook reference](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks).
The real 2.1.310 probe exposed older Task payloads without tool-event agent ids. Those events need
an exact chapter-owned path or Studio VIDEO+CLIP command in the registered session; the log records
path association without inventing a caller. Shared and generic activity is dropped there. Newer
agent-id payloads can attribute generic tools too. No transcript is opened in either path.

The absent-record path checks one file and starts no interpreter, Nix or kit CLI: measured under
suite load at 4.54ms median / 5.23ms p95 including the command shell. Expired and unrelated-session
records stay below 30ms p95. Activity bursts coalesce at two seconds and flush their final pending
action on stop. Completion archives bindings and removes the active record. Quiet/stalled rules
remain 30/120 seconds; “finished” describes agent completion without claiming its checks passed.
Codex and unhooked sessions retain explicit events. A real one-subagent Haiku probe delivered four
automatic events on the desk; naming/payload compatibility required resumes, and its lingering
parent CLI processes were stopped after completion, so their final usage totals are unavailable.

## Code and sound need evidence at the same instant

The code port was compared at twelve matching frames against the original components, including the
wrong character, correction, save, run, folds and payoff. All 1,800 editor/run/window states matched;
the saved 60-second film’s 26 key attacks measured within 0.15 frames of their pictured keys. Code-view
cue dependencies enter the cache key so a timing edit cannot reuse a stale editor view. The browser
now lives in an independent cache, installed only by explicit setup; npm package refreshes do not erase it.

Audio checks compare each typed key with its scheduled sound and correlate its recorded attack with
the saved movie at sample resolution. Four-sample decimation once aliased a 239-sample limiter delay
into false offsets. A masked attack stays unverified. Sparse key-only pieces may need a quieter target
than spoken narration to preserve their dynamics; report the chosen target and measured result. The
finish record stores the gain actually applied, including when the requested target is unreachable.
