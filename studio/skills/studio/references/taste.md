# Taste

## Choose a story the viewer can carry

Default to a canonical undergraduate explanation. Use one small, visible example and few quantities to remember.
Three or four ideas is useful guidance, not a cap: breadth was never the problem; density of
numbers and caveats was. A barrage of precise details can
leave the viewer without a usable model. Put the desired level into the brief as a sentence.

Teach the subject in terms the viewer can use elsewhere. Use canonical names and relate nearby
terms when their distinction matters. Explain the primitive before manipulating it: show a key
and its value, a record, a request or a pixel. Familiarity with a specific project is a poor
substitute for understanding the field. Define each core term when it first matters, as the field's
tools use it or with the simplification said, and
introduce each tool by the problem that created it. Say things literally.

Default to a standard lecture in the subject's natural textbook order: why it exists, what it
is, how it works, the cases that break it, what it costs and where it is used. Give an insight
at each step and local closure to each part. A short opening hook is welcome. A governing
question and a protagonist with a stake are tools for subjects that naturally have them;
never manufacture them to fill a template. Persistent forms still help us follow changing state.

Use the same concrete example across the ideas. Show intermediate states, then generalize.
A plausible wrong intuition can make a useful prediction to test. Leave real thinking time after
a question, usually two or three seconds, so the viewer can try before the answer appears.

Show relationships explicitly. Put two structures holding the same data side by side and explain
what each is for and how each changes over time. Map an analogy part by part. Make “the same,”
“this” and “it” have one visible referent. Say where components run and whether they share a machine.
When a sequence replaces earlier machinery, show what remains, what changes and what disappears.
These details prevent the viewer from building several disconnected explanations.

Keep a lecture about the general subject. Use the user's experience to choose an accessible
example, then explain the canonical mechanism. Replaying their mistakes or incidental project
problems can make the explanation feel narrow and personal.

## Let the picture speak

**Rhythm rule:** after each key point, leave about **1.5–3 seconds of stillness and
silence**. Key points are the ideas named in the story brief and the payoff of each hero moment,
not every sentence. Between sections, leave a longer pause of about **3–4 seconds** and reset
the stage: clear it or pull back to a new composition before the next section starts. Put the
reset after the settled rest, so the point itself has a still picture and a silent voice.

Everywhere else, motion may flow, overlap the narration and move the camera, as long as it shows
what is being said right then. There is no mandatory hold after every small change. Keep **one
new thing at a time** for genuinely new mechanisms; avoid unrelated movements competing for
attention. Related transformations and reframing can develop one idea continuously. Keep
performed code typing in speech pauses, with every key heard, then explain the completed line.

The reason is rhythm: small holds after every change create one even pace without a real rest.
Inside an idea the picture can move freely; when the idea lands, everything stops. A section
break acts like a paragraph break. Mark key points and section ends in the brief and beat table's
`Breath` column, and place `[breath N]` after the corresponding complete narration sentence.
`narrate` adds silence and records the interval; scene authors settle the picture before it and
keep it still throughout. Allow a reset after a section breath, before the next voice starts.

Keep the dead-time warning: a long hold where nothing new arrived and nothing landed is still
dead time; a marked breath is not. The pacing check exempts only the marked rest, not adjacent
empty time, and warns if significant motion crosses a breath. Its short-gap warnings ask whether
genuinely new mechanisms compete, not for extra holds between related moves. Prediction pauses
still belong only after a real question the viewer can answer. Do not add meaningless motion to
silence a diagnostic. Stop repeated demonstrations before introducing the next concept; loop
only when repetition is the subject.

Fill the frame with the subject. Make code, mechanisms and diagrams large enough to inspect at
the delivered resolution. A tiny diagram or small editor surrounded by an empty stage wastes the
picture. Enlarge the subject or move the camera before adding labels. Use close-ups for detail
and return to the overview when the relationship matters.

Keep the overview clean: component names and current state. Open a component into a full-frame
close-up, with its name and a small unlabelled minimap if orientation needs it. Highlight the component
before moving in. Return to the same overview with the relevant state changed; leave close-up
details behind. Reframe the components that exist so early pictures stay balanced.

Move toward the thing being introduced. A recording surface should come close enough to inspect;
a film about the inside of a mechanism should open it. A close camera can make a hero contact
readable and its stake felt. A camera move earns its time by revealing a relationship.

## Choose the look for the subject

**3D admission test — a hard rule:** use 3D only when you can finish the sentence
“the viewer needs to see ___, and the flat diagram hides it.” Record the missing spatial
relationship in the brief. Making an abstraction feel like a physical object is not a reason.
Abstract subjects almost never pass this test. A hash table does not: its addresses, collisions
and comparisons are clearer in a flat drawing than in a lit cabinet.

### Abstract subjects: 2D craft

For data structures, algorithms, code, maths, protocols and systems, the bar is **the director's
own style** ([style sheet](https://claude.ai/artifact/GEtCLD3YnkS3jLb1HiVxMw); his words in
HISTORY.md 0.32.0), not a copy of 3Blue1Brown, one study reference of several. Settled so far:

- **Windows mean a programmer at work**: editor, terminal and browser only while someone types,
  runs or reads. What happens underneath is drawn (diagram shapes are not windows).
- **Pictures as plain as the subject**: no poetic imagery (a river of light) for a slow checkout.
- **Creative freedom where it makes sense**: he finds his style by reacting to the new, so try a
  new visual world where a part would gain from one. No quota; it still shows the mechanism.
  Name it in the brief and ask his verdict. A whole series' own world and cast is the opt-in
  process in `kodawari.md`.

A finished illustration need not have depth or simulated materials. Aim for:

- A calm ground and a small designed palette, with one meaning per colour.
- Clean vector marks and confident line weights; never boxes-and-arrows clip art.
- Beautiful type. Code, numbers and data are first-class graphics that move and transform.
- A large, centred mechanism that fills the frame and is easy to inspect.
- Continuous transformations: a key's characters fold into a number, then that number slides
  into its slot. Preserve identity so the viewer sees how one form becomes the next, not slides.
- Purposeful camera moves: push in on a bucket and pull back to the table. Let reframing follow the current idea; do not hold the same static shot for minutes.
- Visual variety across the film: close-ups, an overview, side-by-side comparisons and a layout
  that changes as the lecture moves on. Reusing a single composition is not visual coherence.

A physical metaphor is allowed only when it maps exactly onto the mechanism; explain the mapping
and limits in the brief. Draw it in the same flat, illustrated style, never model and light it.
Do not invent drawers, machinery or a room to make an abstract subject look substantial.

### Physical subjects: spatial craft

For a hard drive, an engine, a lock or another physical mechanism, Ciechanowski is the bar.
After passing the same 3D admission test, the lit 3D stage and Blender plates are available:
use depth, light, texture and materials to reveal real form, contacts, occlusion and motion.
Kurzgesagt is also a useful illustration reference for physical subjects. Beauty should expose
how the thing works; decorative geometry is not a substitute for the mechanism.

### Set the bar before the shoot

Render three to five final-quality style frames in the real engine, compared beside references as
`story.md` describes, and revise weak frames before shooting. Chapters must match the accepted frames and the
finished film must also earn its camera and layout variety.

Treat geometry and motion as claims. A copy leaves its source in place. A reference stays connected
to its value. Keep a map fixed when copying its record. Show the part the voice names, including
parts behind other geometry. If a gap or speed is exaggerated, show the true relation first or say
the simplification once. A precise-looking model can teach a false mechanism very persuasively.

## Perform the hero moments

Choose moments whose visible action carries the explanation. Build them into the first shoot. Code typed in a real rhythm, a corrected typo, a held breath before running,
real output appearing and a window opening are useful examples. An animated
state change alone misses the opportunity to make the mechanism felt.

On screens, follow `screens.md`: a desktop per kind of work, a tab for more of the same, a
switch sliding between sentences. Keep cause and effect in one shot when possible. Let the viewer see the edit and the program
answering it, the switch and the resulting change, the contact and its consequence. Give a physical event
anticipation, a contact on a named cue and a settle. When sound is permitted, time it from that cue;
a recorded effect lands on its transient peak.

Point on the word, act in the pauses. When the narrator names one of several things, dim the rest
as the word is said (`c.focus`; a screens `point`); a state change lands in the pause after the
sentence. Film grammar is in `grammar.md`.

Default to no sound effects. Sound makes an object real (Ben Burtt): it belongs to things with
a body that are touched. Permit recorded keys for performed typing, clicks, a window opening, or
a real physical contact such as a disk head landing. List the exact permitted events in the shoot
brief; an empty list means silence. Never put a whoosh, pop, ding or chime on an abstraction: a
key entering a bucket, a label appearing or a reveal; a drawer metaphor does not license one.
One trial exception: the outcome sounds, `finished` and `failed`, on outcome marks, when the
director asks.

The recorded sound kit stays. Keep permitted sounds below speech, with their transient on the
pictured frame. Type in human bursts, with punctuation pauses and plausible corrections; vary
key recordings without losing rhythm. Enter, save and run accents must correspond to performed
keys or clicks. Do not add an accent merely because an output appeared. New films get palette
music: wallpaper, steady, far under the voice, never swelling into a pause.

Spend emphasis on meaning. Give the strongest event more space and a closer view when it reveals
the stake. Let the world react only as the mechanism permits. A heavy physical contact can recoil
or hold briefly; a packet needs no invented mass. Use tiny overshoot on UI objects, keep type steady
and give outer contours more weight than internal dividers so objects retain their shape.

Leave unimportant things still: no drifting decorations, particles, pulsing labels or effects
on every arrival. Silence can express a missing mechanism or make the payoff land. Let a loud
action decay before the next word.

## Give every mark a job

Use labels to name things, attached to the thing they name. Keep the stage free of sentences
repeating the narration, footnotes, banners, subtitles under headings and decorative status tags.
A small consistent chapter name can orient the viewer. Put qualifications into the spoken
explanation; tiny disclaimers cannot repair a misleading picture.

Never burn subtitles in; captions are a separate file (`kit.md`). Keep labels comfortably readable at the playback size; 18 px at 1080p is a floor,
and a 540p preview often needs much larger type. Break long code lines or widen the editor before
shrinking its text. Keep status text outside plots, where its position could be mistaken for data.

Use one colour for one meaning throughout the video, across flat and 3D pictures; changing it
forces the viewer to relearn the diagram. Declare the legend and tag shapes with their meaning.
Design a palette for this subject: quiet neutrals and a few deliberate accents.

Use real images, source code, captures and output where they explain the subject. Keep quotes
short and discuss them. Record provenance off screen. Introduce invented example values as
supposed values, not with “simulated” tags.

Give the subject a visual identity through shape, state, framing and, where appropriate, material. Avoid cards
nested inside cards, icons on every point, ornamental strips, gradients, glows, frame borders,
blinking dots and identical fade-and-slide entrances: they make explanations look interchangeable.

For code, start with the smallest useful program and add what resolves the visible limitation.
Collapse the editor between uses. Fold already explained declarations in source order, introduce
external names and highlight a line shortly before discussing it. Show helper details when they
are needed. End on the complete working program so the pieces become one mechanism.

## Speak as a person

The narration is a person talking to one curious viewer, in full spoken sentences with "we",
"you" and "I", contractions, an open question and a callback at the end. [script.md](script.md)
holds the principles, the tics to cut, before-and-after pairs from our own films and the
read-aloud revision method. Put intuition before notation and say parameter names as words.
Over a rich picture the voice can be lighter: explain why, cost and failure, without describing
the visible action. End with the mechanism working and a useful consequence, then give its final
key point a still, silent breath.

Keep production, tools used to make the film, briefs and private context out of the narration.
Subject-specific product names and code belong when the viewer needs them. This keeps the
explanation useful on its own.

Keep one voice and speed throughout. Use paragraph synthesis so intonation carries across
sentences. Let punctuation and deliberate holds provide rhythm. Check names, acronyms and isolated
letters against the lexicon: “A reads one” must pronounce the letter, and “ID” must sound as intended.
A late voice change moves every event the picture was timed to.

Let the video breathe. Spend time on the idea and its visible consequence; a denser script is not
more helpful merely because its facts are correct.

## Notes that became rules

After each video, add a short entry to `taste/notes.md` only when the user's own note establishes a
lasting preference. Keep the note's wording, the resulting imperative and the reason. Link the video
and note so a future change can understand the context. Fold the lesson into the relevant paragraph
above, which is what later films read. Keep temporary fixes and reviewer opinions in the video's
review files.
